Why JEPA For Materials Property Prediction

Predicting representations instead of data, and why that fits materials science.
JEPA
materials

Published

August 10, 2026

Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) is defined by predicting the representation of missing data, not the data itself, a move described in LeCun’s 2022 position paper on autonomous machine intelligence. Mechanically, JEPA has 3 parts. First, a context encoder taking available data and producing an embedding. Second, a target encoder which produces an embedding of a masked chunk of data that the model tries to match. Third, a predictor, which takes the context embedding and some positional or mask tokens telling the predictor where the hidden data sits, and predicts the target embedding. The loss is a simple regression (usually L1/L2) in embedding space, where L1 is the absolute difference, and L2 is the Euclidean distance.

Two design details do a lot of heavy lifting in making JEPA viable: the stop gradient on the target, and the exponential moving average relating the target encoder to the context encoder. The stop gradient makes it so that the target encoder is treated as only a regression target; gradients will not flow on that branch. This is done to prevent representation collapse. To keep updating the target, though, we relate the target and context with an EMA relation, adjusting the target encoder’s weights minutely for a change in the context encoder’s weights, allowing the teacher to grow according to the student’s improvements. Because the target encoder can decide what the target embedding looks like and improve that embedding in iterations, the model can learn to drop the unpredictable and keep only what is useful: the meaning of the target instead of everything about it. This is the entire benefit of the JEPA design: Encouraging representation that captures predictable and task-relevant information and structure, while discarding variability that is difficult or unnecessary to predict. Compared against MAE, which is forced to reproduce every detail, no matter its relevance, and contrastive methods, which need carefully engineered pairs, JEPA is less costly in both compute and labour.

The JEPA lineage starts with I-JEPA. It takes an image, then tokenizes it into patches with a ViT. It samples one context block and several target blocks, encodes the context, and for each target block, feeds the context embedding plus the target block’s positional tokens into the predictor and regresses onto the EMA target encoder’s embedding of that block. Because prediction happens over blocks at a semantic level, the representations it learns are strong for downstream linear-probe and fine-tuning tasks, and it trains faster than pixel-reconstruction MAE at comparable quality. It is non-generative and non-contrastive, which means no decoder, pixel loss, negatives, or hand-designed augmentations. This is good news for predicting scientific data where “sensible” augmentations often don’t exist. The masking scale for the target block is a hyperparameter with physical meaning: make the target blocks too small, and the task collapses to low-level interpolation; too large and it becomes unpredictable. Thus, tuning the predictability band becomes the task at hand.

The next prominent iteration of JEPA is V-JEPA 2. V-JEPA 2 predicts future states in a learned representation space, forcing the target encoder to represent motion and dynamics instead of pixels. It predicts a handful of latent features per frame instead of rendering a full image. This does have a tradeoff in that its outputs are only meaningful when fed back into a planner or a downstream probe. The ability for humans to view the prediction is given in exchange for a prediction that is cheap, abstract, and dynamically oriented rather than visually.

But, as said before, JEPA also has an advantage in predicting scientific data, and the area I focus on now is Materials Science. Models in this lineage include graph JEPA, which shares many similarities with GNNs, showing that the JEPA recipe transfers well to graphs. From there, there are also polymer JEPA, fragment-based molecular JEPA, and Crys-JEPA.

Polymer JEPA pretrains on polymer molecule graphs, then fine-tunes on downstream property prediction tasks. This model found that JEPA self-supervised pretraining enhances downstream performance, particularly when labelled data is very scarce, with improvements across all tested datasets. Concretely, they found an optimal context size around 60% of the full graph: roughly 10–15 atoms of a 20–25 atom polymer graph. The model is robust across a 50–80% context range, with degradation only when the context gets very small.

Next, fragment-based molecular JEPA improves on the usual JEPA methodology by predicting fragments, such as functional groups, rather than node- and graph-level predictions. Node-level information and structures are usually blind to higher-order molecular dynamics and semantics, while graph-level approaches overlook fine-grained structures. The compromise is to predict at the fragment level in embedding space, which also serves as the correct unit for conveying meaning in chemistry.

Crys-JEPA is the most distinctive in this lineage, as it does not solely rely on JEPA methodology. Instead, it takes advantage of JEPA’s low cost to use it as a screening method for more expensive yet more precise and accurate Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations, saving time and compute. This model pretrains JEPA with an InfoNCE objective to build a crystal latent space, organized around formation energy. Similar to contrastive methods, formation energies with a small difference are grouped close by in the latent space, while larger differences appear farther apart. Thus, when prediction embeddings come through the model, their representation in latent space also conveys information on the general stability of the embedded crystal, saving much effort from DFT, allowing compute to be focused towards crystals which aren’t obviously unstable.

The latent space approach does not beat a coordinates approach in every metric, but it does have its advantages, especially in materials science. JEPA’s embeddings represent semantic meaning, discarding coordinates but preserving properties. This means latent predictions are more efficient in achieving the goal of property prediction under label scarcity, a very real condition in the field of materials science. Meanwhile, symmetry-aware coordinate methods are much better at generating structures and placing atoms. This means that, to maximize effect, an ideal system would combine both approaches, with coordinate methods used for generation and JEPA used for screening to lower costs while maintaining quality.

Based on these qualities, the direction I’m taking is a hierarchical JEPA for biomaterials property prediction, where the levels mirror the physical scales of the material: atoms to functional groups to molecular representation to the protein-adsorption interface.
